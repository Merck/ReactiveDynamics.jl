ReactiveDynamics.jl Modeling Copilot

A guided workflow that takes a capacity-constrained business decision from a conversation to a checked, reproducible model and a number you can defend — without anyone on the team writing a line of code.

The analyst describes the decision. The copilot builds the model, tries to break it, and reports a number that says what it rests on, and the seed that replays it. Decisions of this kind — a launch-capacity reservation, a bid under a staffing ceiling, a portfolio that competes for the same people — today need a modeler, a quarter, and trust in a spreadsheet nobody else can read. The copilot turns that into a guided session an analyst or a strategy lead can run, with every step checked by the engine rather than by the reader's faith.

The expensive part of a capacity decision is not the arithmetic. It is getting the question right, getting the operation right, and knowing which parts of the answer you are entitled to defend in the room. Those steps are why these questions go to a specialist, take weeks, and come back as a spreadsheet the decision-maker has to take on faith.

The copilot does that work with you. It interviews you about the decision, turns your description of the operation into a running model of it, stress-tests that model against what you said the business does, and only then reports a number, together with the mechanism that produced it and an honest account of how much evidence stands behind it. The same capability applies to the next question, and the one after that: this is a way of working, not a one-off model.

1 · The value: decisions at scale, without a modeling bottleneck

More decisions get a real model. Today a queueing question — who waits when the line is full, what a reservation is worth before the readout, what twelve more people buy — gets a model only when it is big enough to justify a specialist. Most get a spreadsheet, and a spreadsheet cannot represent a queue: contention is an interaction between rows, and its rows are independent by construction. The copilot lowers the cost of a real model to a conversation, so the threshold drops.

The person who owns the decision stays in the loop. The interview is conducted with the analyst or the executive, in business language. Every assumption lands in a written brief they agreed to — including which inputs were their own estimates and what they expect the answer to be — so the result is compared against the expectation rather than quietly replacing it.

The answer is defensible, not just plausible. Each number arrives with the evidence behind it (a spread and a conservative floor when the options were run as ensembles, the named example paths when they were not), the seed that reproduces it, and the command that produced it. When the CFO asks "what if the second line costs more", the answer is one edited input and a re-run, not a rebuilt workbook.

It compounds. A study is a directory — the brief, the model, the health report, the briefing, every command. The next question about the same operation starts from that model rather than from a blank sheet, and the method is the same whoever runs it.

It opens with an interview, not a template. The first step asks what a good modeler would ask: which decision is actually on the table, what the alternative really is (not a straw man), what is scarce, what happens to work that cannot start yet, how long each step takes and how often it fails. Numbers the user is unsure of are written down as estimates, and the published audit of each captured session says which of them were tested and which were not.

The comparison is designed before it is run. What a decision needs is the difference between two futures. Each future becomes an arm — the same model with one thing changed — so the answer is measured as a difference against its noise, not argued between two separately built spreadsheets.

The model has to survive being doubted. If nothing in the operation is actually contended, there is no queue to simulate, and the copilot says so and stops.

3 · Why the number can be trusted

The model is data, not code. The operation ends up as a structured document in a closed vocabulary the engine understands — places that hold resources, transitions that consume them over time, rates, costs, what each arm changes. Nothing in it is a program. That single design choice is what makes the rest possible:

  • Checked before it runs. A pure validator reads the document and returns a list of problems, each with the exact path of what is wrong. A malformed model does not produce a plausible wrong number; it produces diagnostics. That is what makes it safe to put a language model in the authoring seat: it proposes, a deterministic checker disposes, and the loop repeats until the checker is silent.
  • Nothing is executed that was generated. No eval, no generated scripts, no shell in the model. If a study needs custom logic it is one declared file, announced in the run log.
  • Checked again, for meaning. A schema cannot tell you that your bottleneck never binds or that the horizon cuts off the payoff. Nine plausibility checks do, and a failure blocks the headline.
  • Reproducible by construction. A run is fixed by the model and a seed. Options are compared on the same random outcomes, so a difference is the option, not the luck.
  • Honest about uncertainty. Every published figure says what it rests on: an ensemble with a spread and a floor, or a stated handful of paths. A difference that cannot be told from noise is reported as a tie rather than rounded into a story.
  • Guarded in CI. The reference the copilot reads is generated from the engine itself and fails the build if it drifts, and a known-answer test re-derives a published result through the same path on every change.

Contrast with a code-writing assistant. An assistant using a general simulation library writes code, and code cannot be checked before it runs: it crashes or it returns something, and something is the dangerous case. Here the machine can author the model, be wrong, be told exactly where, and try again — and a reviewer who reads no code at all can still audit the result.

4 · What it is made of

The copilot is a thin layer of procedure over an open-source engine that checks everything. Skills carry the procedure for each phase — what to ask, what to check, how to report. A single command-line tool, rdx, does the work: validate, run, diagnose, run ensembles, measure the effect of an option, draw the network. Underneath is ReactiveDynamics.jl, a stochastic, resource-constrained process engine built on AlgebraicAgents.jl.

How you use it today. Open a session in Claude Code on a checkout of the repository and say what you are deciding. The rd-study skill takes over: it runs the interview, writes the brief, authors the model, runs the checks, and asks for your sign-off at each gate. You never type a command; you see each one, and its result, in the conversation.

How you will use it next. The MCP server (W8) exposes the same six operations as tools with a warm Julia session, so any MCP host — Claude Desktop included — can drive a study without a terminal. The plugin (W12) makes installation one step. None of this changes what a study is; it changes who can start one.

5 · A real session, from question to answer

The question. A VP of launch readiness has two Phase-3 biologics reading out nine months apart, one sterile fill-finish line that can carry one launch but maybe not two, a CMO term sheet that expires on 30 November 2026, and a CFO who says not to pay for capacity on a 45% asset. Reserve a CMO slot now, build a second line, or wait? The operator arrived leaning toward a reservation sized for Tern.

What the copilot did. In three rounds of questions it recovered the constraint the operator said “people forget”: a fill site must run three validation batches before the filing, or wait six months for a supplement. It then built one model with an arm per option, checked it against the operator's own calibration figures ($250.0M of Kestrel revenue in its first year against their ~$250M), and compared the options on paired paths, where the same seed gives every option the same readout dates and lead times.

What it found.

  • Reserve the slot now, usable by either product, and review it at Kestrel's readout: +$3.8M ± $1.1M against waiting at the stated odds, and +$25M if a batch earns its peak-year value.
  • The value is supply protection. If both succeed (27%), the reservation is worth about +$39M. Waiting leaves Tern short at launch on 26 of 37 paths, costing on average 11 batches and $51M. Otherwise the reservation is a bounded $9–11M cost.
  • The operator's prior was overturned. A Tern-only reservation leaves Kestrel short on every path where both succeed.
  • Their other prior was confirmed. A second line is $73–105M worse than waiting even when both succeed.

The moment worth watching. The first pass came out at roughly break-even, because a rigid reservation carried a $72M tail when only Tern succeeds. The model made that tail visible, and the operator supplied the fix: Kestrel reads out nine months earlier, so drop the reservation if Kestrel fails. The copilot encoded the rule, re-ran 30 paired paths and reported the result, including where the operator's guess was right.

How to read the evidence. Every comparison is a set of paired single-seed paths, weighted by the stated odds. No option-level ensembles were run, because a 96-seed ensemble took 53 minutes (tracked as W24). The session was blind-captured by two agents that could not see each other's reasoning, and the operator's pre-commitment fact sheet is published with an audit. That audit leads with what the interview left untested: demand ±30%, marketed growth and readout correlation.
