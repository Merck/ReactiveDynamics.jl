ReactiveDynamics.jl Modeling Copilot

A guided workflow that takes a business decision whose answer depends on how the operation behaves over time — shared capacity, lead times, feedback, decisions taken mid-course — from a conversation to a checked, reproducible model and a number you can defend. The analyst describes; nobody writes the model by hand.

The analyst describes the decision. The copilot builds the model, tries to break it, and reports a number that says what it rests on and can be replayed exactly. Decisions of this kind — a launch-capacity reservation, a bid under a staffing ceiling, a portfolio whose funding follows its own results, a service backlog that feeds back into demand — today need a modeler, weeks of work, and trust in a workbook nobody else can read. The copilot turns that into a guided session with the person who owns the decision, with each step checked by the engine rather than by the reader's faith. This page sets out the vision, what exists today, and the direction.
what it is a reusable modeling capability: an interview, a running model of how your operation behaves, a health pass that can refuse, and a briefing that ships as a generated report — run in conversation
who drives it an analyst or a strategy lead, in business language. Today it runs in Claude Code on a checkout of the repository with Julia installed, so someone technical sets it up; the direction is a tool any assistant can call (see §5)
why it can be trusted the model is a checked data document in a closed vocabulary, never code — validated before it runs, reproducible from a seed afterwards
the case on this page a launch-supply decision in pharma, captured with AI on both sides: the operator was a role-played agent answering from a fact sheet sealed before the first message. The company is invented; the numbers are real runs of the model, on paired single-seed paths, not ensembles

The expensive part of a decision like this is not the arithmetic. It is getting the question right, getting the operation right, and knowing which parts of the answer you are entitled to defend in the room. That is why these questions go to a specialist, take weeks, and come back as a workbook the decision-maker has to take on faith.

The copilot does that work with the decision owner. It interviews them about the decision, turns their description of the operation into a running model, stress-tests the model against what they said the business does, and only then reports a number — with the mechanism that produced it and an honest account of the evidence behind it. The same capability serves the next question and the one after: a way of working, not a one-off model.

1 · More decisions get a real model, and the owner stays in the loop

The threshold for a real model drops. Today a question about how an operation behaves — who waits when the line is full, what a reservation is worth before the readout, what happens to intake when the backlog grows — gets a model only when it is big enough to justify a specialist. The rest get a workbook. A workbook can hold a monthly time series and even a Monte Carlo draw, but it cannot easily hold one shared line allocated month by month between products whose launch dates are themselves random, plus a rule that changes the plan when a readout lands. Building that in a spreadsheet means writing a simulator inside it that nobody can check. In the case on this page, a sheet built on average dates has no path on which a late qualification delays a filing, so it could not have shown the $72M loss on one path that made the operator change the plan.

The owner stays in the loop. The interview runs in business language with the person who owns the decision. Every assumption lands in a written brief they agree to, including which inputs were their own estimates and what they expect the answer to be, so the result is compared against the expectation rather than quietly replacing it.

The answer is defensible, not just plausible. Each number arrives with the evidence behind it, the command that produced it and the seed that replays it. When the CFO asks "what if the second line costs more", the answer is one edited input and a batch of re-runs the same day, not a rebuilt workbook.

It compounds. A study is a directory — brief, model, health report, briefing, every command — and it ships as a generated report the modeler can forward as is. The next question about the same operation starts from that model.

2 · The engine models more than queues; the copilot's checks are catching up

The engine underneath is a general process simulator. Contention for shared capacity is the pattern the first studies exercised, and it is where the copilot's checks are sharpest. It is one of several patterns a model can carry, often in the same model:

PatternBusiness exampleCopilot today
Contentiontwo launches on one fill line; a bench of scientistsauthored and checked end to end
Lead times and clockstech transfer, a build, a regulatory reviewauthored and checked
Feedbacka backlog that throttles intake; share lost after a stock-out; funding that follows resultsauthored and run — the case model has 12 rates that read the live state — but no check yet tests loop behaviour

The interview's qualifying test and the plausibility checks were written for contention. Widening them to feedback and coupled models is part of the direction in §5 (W25).

3 · Four steps, and a check that can stop the answer

If the question is arithmetic, the interview says so and stops. Nothing shared, nothing delayed, nothing reacting: a spreadsheet is the right tool. Numbers the owner is unsure of are recorded as estimates, and the published audit of each captured session says which of them were tested.

A failed check is not a footnote. In the case, every option failed one check — some steps never fired on a single run — because an option that never builds a second line has nothing to put through it. The study reported a number only after DOCTOR.md explained each dead step, and a flag that lets an option declare its intentionally dead steps is on the tracker (W20). A horizon warning also stands: both products are still ramping at end-2032, so every value is a within-horizon number, and the report says so.

4 · The number can be trusted because the model is data, not code

The operation ends up as a structured document in a closed vocabulary the engine understands — places that hold resources, transitions that consume them over time, rates, rules, costs, what each option changes. Nothing in the document is a program, and nothing in it can name one. That is what makes the rest possible:

  • Checked before it runs. A pure validator reads the document and returns every problem with its exact location. A malformed model produces diagnostics, not a plausible wrong number. That is what makes it safe to put a language model in the authoring seat: it proposes, a deterministic checker disposes, and the loop repeats until the checker is silent.
  • Checked again, for meaning. A schema cannot tell you that your bottleneck never binds or that the horizon cuts off the payoff. The plausibility checks do.
  • Reproducible by construction. A run is fixed by the model and a seed; options are compared on the same random outcomes.
  • Guarded in CI. The reference the copilot reads is generated from the engine and fails the build if it drifts, and a known-answer test re-derives a published result through the same path on every change.

Where the guarantee stops today. It covers the model, not all the arithmetic around it. In the case, the documents were emitted by a short generator script, each then validated as data; and the odds-weighting behind the headline ran in analysis scripts the assistant wrote during the session. Those scripts are published with the study and were re-run independently over the stored runs, and every figure matched — but they are code, reviewed by reading. Moving that weighting into the engine's own effect command is part of the direction (W27).

Contrast with a code-writing assistant. An assistant using a general simulation library writes the model as code, and code cannot be checked before it runs: it crashes or returns something, and something is the dangerous case. Here the machine can author the model, be wrong, be told exactly where, and try again.

5 · What exists today, and where it is going

The copilot is a thin layer of procedure over an open-source engine. Skills carry the procedure for each phase — what to ask, what to check, how to report. A single command-line tool, rdx, does the work: validate, run, diagnose, run ensembles, measure the effect of an option, draw the network. Underneath is ReactiveDynamics.jl, a stochastic process engine built on AlgebraicAgents.jl.

Today. The engine, the four-phase workflow as five skills, the rdx tool, the generated schema reference with its drift gate, the known-answer test, the generated study report, and four published studies, three with their captured sessions. A study runs in Claude Code on a checkout of the repository: the rd-study skill runs the interview, writes the brief, authors the model, runs the checks and asks for sign-off at each gate, and every command and its result is visible in the conversation.

The direction, in the order it retires risk — first what makes the number more trustworthy, then what widens who can reach it:

DirectionWhat it changesTracker
Fast ensemblesan option-level ensemble in minutes rather than 53 minutes, so studies stop falling back to paired pathsW24
Declared dead stepsan option declares what it intentionally never fires, so a failed check means a bugW20
Weighting in the engineforced outcomes and odds-weighting inside rdx effect, not in session scriptsW27
A critica separate agent audits the briefing's prose against the numbers — the class of error this case retracted out loudW9
Beyond contentiona qualifying test and checks for feedback and mid-course decisions; coupled models as documentsW25

MCP serverthe same operations as tools any assistant can call, with no terminalW8

None of this changes what a study is. The first half makes the answer harder to get wrong; the second half moves the copilot from something a developer sets up to something an analyst opens.

6 · The case: do we pay for capacity before the readouts?

The question. A VP of launch readiness has two late-stage biologics reading out nine months apart (Kestrel, 60% odds; Tern, 45%), one sterile fill-finish line that can carry one launch but maybe not two, a contract-manufacturer (CMO) offer that expires on 30 November 2026, and a CFO who says not to pay for capacity on a 45% asset. Reserve the CMO slot now, build a second line, or wait? The operator arrived leaning toward a reservation sized for Tern.

How it was captured. Both sides were AI. The operator was a role-played agent that wrote its facts to a sheet before the first message and answered only from it; the assistant started from an empty context and could not see the sheet. The sheet is published next to the transcript with an audit of what the interview missed.

What the copilot did. In three rounds of questions it recovered the constraint the operator said "people forget": a fill site must run three validation batches before the filing, or wait six months for a supplement. It built one model with a version per option and checked that the operator's figures went in right: it reproduces their ~$250M of Kestrel first-year revenue and 54 batches a year at peak. Both follow from the inputs by construction, so this is a consistency check, not evidence that the model is right; that is what the plausibility checks are for. It then compared the options on paired paths, with readout outcomes forced and weighted by the stated odds.

What it found.

  • Reserve the slot now, usable by either product, and review it at Kestrel's readout: +$3.8M ± $1.1M against waiting at the portfolio committee's odds, and +$25M if a batch earns its peak-year value.
  • At the operator's own, lower odds for Kestrel (50%), it is break-even: +$1.6M ± $0.9M, just failing the brief's test. The recommendation is supply protection, not a bet on the odds.
  • If both succeed (27%), the reservation is worth about +$39M. Waiting leaves Tern short at launch on 26 of 37 paths and loses Tern demand on all 37, on average 11 batches worth $51M. In every other outcome the reservation is a bounded $9–11M cost.
  • A second line is $73–105M worse than waiting even when both succeed, confirming the operator's view.
  • A Tern-only reservation is safe only with a second CMO contract in writing. Without one it lost $72.5M when both succeed (3 paths, indicative), and the operator took it off the table.

The moment worth watching. The first pass came out at roughly break-even, because a rigid reservation carried a $72M loss on one path where only Tern succeeds. The model made that tail visible, and the operator supplied the fix: Kestrel reads out nine months earlier, so drop the reservation if Kestrel fails. The copilot encoded the rule, re-ran 30 paired paths and reported the result. The excerpt below is that exchange; the full transcript is agent/sessions/launch_supply.html.

What the modeler sends on is the generated report, agent/studies/launch_supply/REPORT.pdf (18 pages): the answer and decision rule, the evidence, why a spreadsheet cannot produce it, the model in the operator's words with the one change that is the option, the execution map, and provenance down to the commit and all 208 stored runs. It is built from the study's files by agent/scripts/render_report.jl and never edited by hand.

7 · What this does not show
  • That a real person answers like the role-played one. Both sides of the case were AI, and the operator was cooperative. It demonstrates the workflow, not anyone's supply plan.
  • Ensemble-grade evidence. Every comparison rests on paired single-seed paths, because a 96-seed ensemble took 53 minutes (W24).
  • That the soft inputs were tested. The interview recorded demand ±30%, marketed growth and readout correlation, then tested none of them. The published audit leads with them.
  • That the briefing's prose is checked. The numbers are generated from runs; the prose is written by the model. The assistant caught and retracted two of its own claims in this case; a critic that does this systematically is W9.
  • That every pattern is checked equally. Contention is checked end to end; feedback and mid-course decisions ran here without loop checks; coupled models do not yet go through the copilot (W25).
  • That the planned pieces exist. The outlined boxes in §5 are the direction, not shipped.

Phase 4 of 4, the final result. Your guess holds: under the review rule, the Tern-only outcome costs $9.4M against waiting, and the $72M tail is gone.

The rule: reserve the CMO now and review at Kestrel's readout. If Kestrel fails, Tern's validation batches move in-house, Tern files on our own line, we give the CMO notice and pay the forfeit. If Kestrel succeeds, nothing changes.

  • Tern-only outcome, 30 paired paths: −$9.4M ± $0.02M against waiting. Every path falls between −$9.7M and −$9.3M.
  • It is pure cost. Tern's revenue is the same as under waiting on all 30 paths, Tern files on time on all 30, and no Tern demand is lost on any of them.
  • The old rule (keep the CMO no matter what) lost $31.7M ± $6.0M in this outcome over 10 paths. Four of those 10 lost more than $20M, the worst lost $72.1M, and 4 of 10 filed late.

  • At the peak value per batch: +$25.0M ± $2.8M.

The remaining downside is also in that outcome: 3 of 37 paths lose money, the worst $14.3M. That happens when Tern reads out before the CMO has qualified and has to wait for it.

The late-filing mismatch is resolved. It was sampling chance, not a model error.

  • I re-ran three members of the 96-seed baseline with their exact seeds. They reproduce its late-filing months and lost Tern demand exactly, with or without the outcomes forced.
  • The value gap is a constant 0.72% from a one-month discounting convention, which cancels out of every comparison.
  • Our 13 late filings in 37 paths against the baseline's 2 in 20 fits chance (p = 0.06). Pooled, the rate is 15 in 57 (range 0.17–0.39). The two sources do differ on how long a late filing runs: 2–3 months in our paths, 2 months in both of the baseline's cases.
  • Late filing is about as likely either way: 0.10 overall under reserve-and-review, 0.095 under waiting.
  • The figures are usable now, but quote them with their range: they are the least precise numbers in the report.

Backup qualification: I did not model it at your $0.5M a batch. The model charges $0.15M a batch for in-house validation, so its cost in the Tern-only outcome is understated by about $1M. That is arithmetic, not a simulated result.
