A simulated session, captured turn by turn: an operations manager arrives with a budget question and no model, and leaves with a number they can defend. Rendered from the actual exchange — nothing here was written as illustration.

Two laptops, not two technicians. The manager arrived weighing three options and led with headcount: “whether to hire 2 more techs, buy more diagnostic equipment, or just change how we prioritize jobs.” At 48 seeds the model ranks them in almost the reverse order. Two more technicians avoid $57,504 ± $21,741 of SLA penalties a year at a cost of $190,000 a year — a net loss of about $132k. Two more diagnostic laptops avoid $228,050 ± $18,712 a year for $38,000 once: four times the benefit for a fifth of the cost. Phase 1 of 4 — framing. Before I build anything, let me check your question against the one test that decides whether a simulation is worth the six weeks or whether I should just do arithmetic with you on a whiteboard in twenty minutes.

A simulation only earns its cost when three things are true at once:

  • Something runs out. Your 7 technicians. Yes.
  • Work occupies it for a while. A repair holds a tech for hours or days before the analyzer is fixed. Yes.
  • When jobs collide, something has to choose who waits. Yes — and the fact that "change how we prioritize" is one of your three candidate levers tells me this rule is real, contested, and currently undocumented.

Three for three, so this is a genuine simulation question. Here's why it matters that it's all three: the thing you're actually asking for — what does the 8th and 9th technician buy me — is not a property of a technician. It's a property of your system under contention. There's no formula for it, which is exactly why the backlog debate keeps going in circles. Two out of three and I'd have sent you away with a spreadsheet.

One thing I'll flag now, because it's the most common way studies like this go wrong: it is entirely possible that technicians are not what's actually binding. If jobs sit waiting on parts or on a shared diagnostic kit, hiring 2 techs buys you two people standing around, and the model will say so. I'd rather find that out in week one than have you defend the wrong number in week six.

So, three questions. I've proposed a default for each — if a default is close enough, just say "yes" and we move.

1. Demand and duration. These two numbers together decide whether 7 techs bind at all. Roughly how many new broken-analyzer calls come in per week, and how long does one job tie up one tech, door to door, including travel? My placeholder: 30 calls/week, and 1.5 days of one tech per job.

2. What else does a job wait on? Besides a free tech — how many of the diagnostic kits do you have today, and does a job need one for its whole duration or just the first visit? If parts are actually the main wall, tell me and this study changes shape.

3. What number do you need to hold up at the budget meeting? The options that usually matter here are: average jobs waiting in backlog, number of SLA breaches per quarter, or average days a customer analyzer is down. My placeholder: SLA breaches avoided, with average backlog as the supporting number, over a 12-month horizon in daily steps. And tell me your SLA clock — mine is a guess: on site and fixed within 3 business days.
